# An explicit fourth-power obstruction for the mixed orbit

On the orbit of \((7,4)\), let \(m\) be a positive odd integer, use the signed primitive slope \(s/t=x(P_m)\), and put
\[
Y_m=s^2+2t^2=81A_m^2,\qquad V_m=(s-t)^2+t^2 .
\]
Here \(A_m>0\). At every odd \(m\) one has the exact character identity
\[
\left(\frac{A_m}{353}\right)=\left(\frac{2}{m}\right).
\]
The symbol on the right is the Jacobi symbol, with value one at \(m=1\). In particular **every prime index \(\ell\equiv5\pmod8\)** has a prime \(p\mid A_\ell\) with odd exponent. If \(Y_\ell\) is cubeful, \(v_p(Y_\ell)\ge6\). This applies to every term of the growing prime-cutoff construction in this class. The obstruction also covers explicit classes of inert indices \(7\bmod8\), as detailed below. It does not determine whether an odd exponent of \(A_\ell\) equals one. ## The exact rational denominator

Use \(E:v^2=u^3-8u^2+8u\), the seed \(P=2(1,1)\), and the isogeny with kernel generated by \((0,0)\). Its target and formulas are
\[
 E':v'^2=u'^3+16u'^2+32u',\qquad
 (u',v')=(u-8+8/u,\ v(1-8/u^2)).
\]
With \(j=\sqrt{-2}\), take the branch \(W=(-2j,8)\) on \(E\) above slope \(j\). The point \(Q=P-W\) has coordinates \(((224-34j)/81,(-680-2240j)/729)\); its image is the rational point \(R=(-200/81,-1360/729)\). Translating \(W\) by \((0,0)\) gives its conjugate. The involution on the elliptic model is \(B\mapsto 2W-B\). Thus \(u'(\phi(B-W))\) is a rational function of the slope \(x=x(B)\), with simple poles precisely at \(x=\pm j\). Its values at infinity and zero are both \(-8\), obtained at \(B=O,(0,0)\), and its seed value is \(-200/81\). These three evaluations give
\[
 u'([m]R)=-8\,\frac{(s-t)^2+t^2}{s^2+2t^2}.
\]
Indeed \(P_m-W=[m](P-W)\). The two integer norms are coprime and odd. The square root of the reduced denominator of \(u'([m]R)\) is therefore exactly \(9A_m\).

The same formula works for the ambient label \(N=2n-1\) at the point \(n(1,1)\). Since \(2W=(1,1)\), its difference from \(W\) is \([N]W\). Here \(R_0=\phi(W)=(-8,16)\) and \([3]R_0=R\). Thus the positive square root of the denominator of \(u'([N]R_0)\) is \(\sqrt{Y_N}\) when \(Y_N,V_N\) are coprime and odd; the original family uses \(N=3m\).

Let \(\psi_n\) denote the usual division polynomial on \(E'\). For positive odd \(m\) the identity with sign and powers of two is
\[
 A_m=(-1)^{(m-1)/2}\,
 \frac{9^{m^2-1}\psi_m(R)}{2^{5(m^2-1)/4}}. \tag{1}
\]
To check all odd primes, use the homogeneous abscissa formulas for multiplication by \(m\). At good reduction their two forms have no common projective zero. If the initial abscissa has reduced denominator \(B_1^2\), the square root of the denominator after multiplication thus has valuation \(m^2v_p(B_1)+v_p(\psi_m(R))\). Here \(B_1=9\); only 2 is bad.

For the valuation at 2 take \(c=2^{5/4}\) in a finite extension of \(\mathbf Q_2\), and set \(D_n=(9/c)^{n^2-1}\psi_n(R)\). The usual division recursions are homogeneous under this scaling. The valuations of \(D_1,D_2,D_3,D_4\), normalized by \(v(2)=1\), are \(0,5/4,0,9/4\). Inductively every odd term is a unit and every even term belongs to \(D_2\mathcal O\). Indeed
\[
 D_{2k+1}=D_{k+2}D_k^3-D_{k-1}D_{k+1}^3,\qquad
 D_{2k}=\frac{D_k}{D_2}(D_{k+2}D_{k-1}^2-D_{k-2}D_{k+1}^2).
\]
In the odd formula just one summand is a unit. In the even formula, if \(k\) is odd both squared factors are divisible by \(D_2^2\); if \(k\) is even both factors \(D_{k\pm2}\) are divisible by \(D_2\). This proves the claimed power of two.

For the sign, the three real roots of the Weierstrass cubic are \(-8-4\sqrt2,-8+4\sqrt2,0\). The abscissa of \(R\) is on the bounded real oval. In the product for \(\psi_m\) over nonzero \(m\)-torsion modulo sign, the \((m-1)/2\) pairs of real torsion points lie on the real identity component and have abscissa at least zero. They contribute negative factors. Points satisfying \(\bar T=-T\) with nonzero purely imaginary ordinate are real on the negative quadratic twist; its odd real torsion is in its identity component. Their original abscissas are at most \(-8-4\sqrt2\), and the factors are positive. The other abscissas occur in nonreal conjugate pairs. The leading coefficient of \(\psi_m\) is \(m>0\), proving (1).

## The local characters

At 353, let \(W_n=\psi_n(R)\bmod353\). The reduction of \(R\) has order eight, with \([4]R=(0,0)\). The first nine \(W_n\) are
\[
 1,158,45,340,320,154,209,0,261.
\]
For a good auxiliary prime \(p\) and reduction order \(r\), the addition formula for division polynomials gives
\[
 W_{n+r}W_{n-r}=-W_{r+1}W_{r-1}W_n^2\pmod p .
\]
If \(r\nmid n\), taking quadratic characters and applying the formula twice shows the character has period dividing \(4r\) in \(n\). Hence a check of the sixteen odd residues modulo 32 suffices at 353. The nonsquare residues are exactly \(m\equiv3,5\bmod8\). Both \(-1\) and 2 are squares modulo 353, so (1) gives the character asserted above.

At 3, for \(3\nmid m\), the leading term of \(\psi_m\) evaluated at \(R\) gives \(9^{m^2-1}\psi_m(R)\equiv m\pmod3\), since \(-200\equiv1\pmod3\) and the lower terms are divisible by 81 after scaling. The exponent on 2 in (1) is even. Thus \(A_m\equiv(-1)^{(m-1)/2}m\pmod3\), excluding \(m\equiv5,7\pmod{12}\).

At 127, the reduction order of \(R\) is 32. Applying (1) and the same character recurrence modulo 128 excludes all \(m\equiv7,15,23\pmod{32}\). Finally an independent test at 89 uses the projective slope. If \(Y_m\) is a fourth power, the coprime conjugates \(s\pm t\sqrt{-2}\) and unique factorization in \(\mathbf Z[\sqrt{-2}]\) imply that \((s+t\sqrt{-2})/(s-t\sqrt{-2})\) is a fourth power. Take \(\sqrt{-2}=40\bmod89\). The point \((1,1)\) on \(E\) has order 27, and \(P_m=[(3m+1)/2](1,1)\). For \(m\equiv\pm1,\pm5,\pm7\pmod{18}\), respectively, the slopes are \(24,21,57\); the ratio values are \(85,53,79\), with 22nd powers \(1,-1,-1\). Thus for primes \(\ell>3\), a fourth power requires \(\ell\equiv\pm1\bmod18\).

Together these four tests imply that **an inert prime \(\ell>3\) with \(Y_\ell\) a fourth power must satisfy \(\ell\equiv-1\pmod{288}\)**. Indeed it must be \(31\bmod32\), and the test at 3 then requires \(\ell\equiv-1\bmod3\), selecting \(-1\bmod9\) from the test at 89. This leaves relative density \(1/48\) among inert primes, by Dirichlet's theorem. The conclusions concern every prime in each excluded class, including primes equal to an auxiliary prime. ## A necessary residue modulo more than a quadrillion

A short further certificate gives an effective lower bound on the index of any remaining fourth power. For each entry \((p,r)\) in the table, \(p\) is an auxiliary prime, \(R\) has reduction order \(r\), and \(4r\mid288k\). Begin with the prime indices \(m\equiv-1\bmod288\). For each of the \(k-1\) possible nonzero residues modulo \(k\), use its representative \(m\bmod288k\) to compute \(\left(\frac{-\psi_m(R)}p\right)\). The minus sign in (1) applies throughout this progression. The only residue for which all characters in its row avoid \(-1\) is \(-1\bmod k\). ## Further perfect powers in the entire class five modulo eight

For every prime \(\ell\equiv5\bmod8\), **\(A_\ell\) is not an \(e\)-th power for any \(2\le e\le22\)**. More generally this holds for every \(e\) having a prime factor at most 19. Thus if \(A_\ell\) is powerful and all its prime exponents are at most 22, their gcd is one and at least two distinct exponents occur.

To verify the odd prime exponents \(q\), use the following auxiliary primes and orders. The character at \(p\) is \(u\mapsto u^{(p-1)/q}\) on nonzero field elements.

| \(q\) | \(p\) | Order \(r\) of the reduction of \(R\) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 13633 | 24 |
| 5 | 31 | 8 |
| 7 | 127 | 32 |
| 11 | 353 | 8 |
| 13 | 189697 | 8 |
| 17 | 2687 | 56 |
| 19 | 191 | 16 |

For \(r\nmid n\), express the character of \(W_{n+jr}\) as an exponent in \(\mathbf F_q\) relative to a generator of the character image. The Ward product relation shows that these exponents have constant second difference as a function of \(j\). Since \(q\) is odd, every such sequence has period dividing \(q\). Each \(r\) in the table is divisible by eight, so the character of \((9^4/32)^{(n^2-1)/4}\) also has period dividing \(qr\) on odd indices. This is the scalar in (1), and its sign is positive throughout \(5\bmod8\).

The verifier enumerates \(n\equiv5\bmod8\) modulo \(qr\) with \(\gcd(n,qr)=1\), using (1) to check that the character is nontrivial throughout. At \(q=3\), for example, the six indices modulo 72 are \(5,13,29,37,53,61\). Their character values alternate between 3042 and 10590 modulo 13633. There are two exceptional primes \(\ell\equiv5\bmod8\) dividing their row's \(qr\). For \((q,\ell)=(5,5),(13,13)\) direct evaluation gives 16 modulo 31 and 155794 modulo 189697, both nontrivial. The quadratic test at 353 supplies the prime exponent two. These obstructions apply even if the putative perfect power has many distinct prime divisors.

## What an arbitrary prime exponent would imply

Let \(\ell>3\) be an inert prime index. In the ambient notation take \(b=\sqrt{Y_\ell^{\rm amb}}\), \(v=\sqrt{V_\ell^{\rm amb}}\) at label \(N=\ell\). At the original seed-orbit index \(m=\ell\), which has ambient label \(3\ell\), the factorization is \(A_\ell=bC_1C_2\) with pairwise coprime positive factors
\[
 3C_1=b^4-4b^2v^2+6v^4,\qquad
 3C_2=b^4+4b^2v^2-2v^4 .
\]
Consequently, if \(A_\ell\) is a \(q\)-th power for an odd prime \(q\), one must have \(b=w^q,C_i=z_i^q\) with pairwise coprime positive integers \(w,z_1,z_2\). Rearranging gives two fixed-coefficient equations
\[
 (w^4)^q-z_2^q=6h^2,\qquad
 9z_1^q-(w^4)^q=2d^2,\qquad
 h=(b^2-v^2)/3,\quad d=b^2-3v^2 .
\]
Indeed \(b^4-C_2=2(b^2-v^2)^2/3\); integrality of the left side gives \(3\mid b^2-v^2\). Also \(9C_1-b^4=2(b^2-3v^2)^2\). Both equations have coprime power bases, and the original coprimality \(\gcd(b,v)=1\) gives \(\gcd(b,h)=\gcd(b,d)=1\), using \(3\nmid b\). The signs and coefficients matter in comparing these equations with published generalized Fermat theorems. [Three-factor proof](abc_octic_mixed_three_factors.md)

Reynolds's paper states finiteness of perfect powers among the actual terms of an elliptic denominator sequence when its first term is divisible by two or three. Here the relevant term for \(R\) is \(B_\ell(R)=9A_\ell\) and \(v_3(B_\ell(R))=2\). In particular \(B_\ell(R)\) fails to be a \(q\)-th power for every odd prime \(q\), regardless of whether \(A_\ell\) is a \(q\)-th power. Using \(R_0\) instead gives \(B_1(R_0)=1\). Thus neither application of that stated theorem bounds the exponent in these two equations. The unbounded odd-prime exponent case remains open in this analysis. [Reynolds, *Perfect powers in elliptic divisibility sequences*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1101.2949)
