# The square denominator from one inert prime

The only primitive solutions of \(a^4+3b^2=4w^4\) with \(w\) a square have \(w=a=1,\ b=\pm1\). This proof uses the rank-one elliptic cover over \(\mathbf Q(\sqrt3)\), the signed slope on the original elliptic curve, and the prime 5. The Mordell–Weil generator of the cover need not be saturated.

## The quadratic cover and its sign

Write \(d=\sqrt3,\ \delta=1+d\), and \(K=\mathbf Q(d)\). On the original curve \(E:Y^2=X^3+36X^2+36X\), use \(P=(-2,8)\) and \(X(nP)=-2r^2\) with \(r=x/y>0\), \(x,y\) relatively prime and odd, \(3\nmid x\), and \(n\) odd. We have \(w=(x^2+3y^2)/4\) and \(F_0=x^4-18x^2y^2+9y^4=-8a^2\). [Original full parametrization](abc_full_quartic_signature.md)

For completeness the sign label in the cover is unique. Put \(H=x^2+2dxy-3y^2\). Its conjugate \(\bar H\) satisfies \(H\bar H=-8a^2\), \(H>\bar H\), hence signs \(+,-\). No odd prime ideal divides both, by their difference \(4dxy\), primitivity, and \(3\nmid x\). The unique dyadic prime is generated by \(\delta\) of norm \(-2\), and \(v_\delta(H)=3\) since \(a\) is odd. The ring \(\mathbf Z[d]\) has class number one and unit group \(\{\pm(2+d)^j\}\). Thus \(H=\delta(2+d)^j t^2\); the signs exclude the extra minus. Reduction at \((d)\) gives \(1=(-1)^j t^2\bmod(d)\), forcing \(j\) even. If \(w\) is square, \(r^2+3\) is a rational square, and consequently \(r\) lifts to
\[
                C:\quad \delta W^2=(r^2+3)(r^2+2dr-3).
\]
Hereafter use \(y_c=\delta W\), so \(y_c^2=\delta F(r)\), \(F(r)=r^4+2dr^3+6dr-9\). At \(r=1\) the two ordinates are \(y_c=\pm4\).

At 2, \(r^2+3=4(z/y)^2\) with \(z=\sqrt w\) odd. Hence \(r^2\equiv1\bmod32\), forcing \(r\equiv\pm1\bmod16\). The cover excludes the negative class. Indeed \(F(-1)=-8\delta\) and \(F'(-1)=-4+12d\) is a multiple of four, so for \(r=-1+16h,\ h\in\mathbf Z_2\), the difference \(F(r)-F(-1)\) belongs to \(64\mathcal O_{K_2}\). Normalizing \(v_2(2)=1\) gives \(v_2(\delta)=1/2\). The ratio of \(F(r)/\delta\) to \(-8\) differs from one by valuation greater than two and is a square in \(K_2=\mathbf Q_2(\sqrt3)\). But \(-8\) is not such a square: the rational squareclasses becoming squares in \(K_2\) are \(1,3\), neither of which is the class \(-2\). Therefore \(r\equiv1\bmod16\).

## Fixing the signed slope at five

At 5, reduction of \(P\) has order eight, with \(4P=(0,0)\bmod5\). The odd multiples have abscissas \(3,2,2,3\) at residues \(1,3,5,7\bmod8\), respectively. The formula \(w=y^2(6-X(nP))/8\) gives square character at the former and nonsquare at the latter. Indeed the values of \(8(6-X)\bmod5\) are 4 at \(X=3\) and 2 at \(X=2\). Change the sign of \(n\), which preserves \(r>0\), to obtain \(n\equiv1\bmod8\). Write \(n=2m+1\); then \(4\mid m\). This is the only preliminary odd-prime congruence test.

We need a common choice of the square root of \(X(nP)/(-2)\) at 2 and 5. For \(T=(X,Y)\in E\), put \(u=(X^2-36)/(2Y)\), \(U=u^2=X(2T)\), \(V=Y(2T)\), \(X_0=-2,Y_0=8\), and
\[
          g(T)=-\frac{X_0V-UY_0}{(X_0-U)X_0u}.
\]
The chord-intercept formula gives \(X(P+2T)=X_0g(T)^2\). With the formal parameter \(t=-X/Y\) at the identity, \(g=1-8t+O(t^2)\) with coefficients in \(\mathbf Z[[t]]\). An explicit integral expansion is given in the signed-slope note. Also, \(g(T+T_0)=g(T)\) for \(T_0=(0,0)\): translation replaces \(X,Y\) by \(36/X,-36Y/X^2\), fixing \(u\), and doubling kills \(T_0\). [Integral expansion](abc_eisenstein_slice_effective_odd_support.md)

At two, \(4P=(25/196,-6245/2744)\) is formal with \(v_2(t)=1\). Since \(4\mid m\), the expansion gives \(g(mP)\equiv1\bmod4\). Therefore \(r=g(mP)\), as \(r\equiv1\bmod16\) and the two rational choices differ by sign. At five, \(mP\) reduces to \(O\) or \(T_0\), and \(g(T+T_0)=g(T)\) together with the same expansion at \(O\) gives \(r\equiv1\bmod5\). This argument applies also to negative \(m\); at \(m=0\) use \(g(O)=1\).

## The two residue disks on the rank-one cover

Put \(s=r-1\). The quartic for \(y_c\) is \(a_4s^4+a_3s^3+a_2s^2+a_1s+e^2\), where
\[
 a_4=\delta,\ a_3=10+6d,\ a_2=24+12d,\ a_1=40+16d,\ e=4 .
\]
Using \(R_+=(1,4)\) as origin identifies \(C\) with
\[
 E_c:\quad Y^2=X^3+(288+144d)X.
\]
Explicitly put
\[
 z=\frac{2e(y_c+e)+a_1s}{s^2},\quad
 X=z+\frac{a_2}{3},\quad
 Y=\frac{(z^2-4e^2a_4)s-a_1z-2e^2a_3}{2e}.
\]
Squaring the definition of \(z\) gives a quadratic equation in \(s\) whose discriminant verifies this model. The map is birational and carries \(dX/(2Y)\) to \(-ds/(2y_c)\), as its expansion at \(s=0,y_c=e\) shows for these two nonzero regular differentials. The other point \(R_-=(1,-4)\) maps to
\[
              D=(21+12d,-153-90d).
\]
The rank-one descent on the 2-isogenous curve with coefficient \(-72-36d\) gives \(\operatorname{rank}E_c(K)=1\). [Descent and local certificates](abc_full_quartic_square_chabauty.md)

Five is inert in \(K\), with residue field \(\mathbf F_{25}=\mathbf F_5(d)\). The curve \(E_c\) has good reduction. The reduction of \(D\) has order four. Modulo 25, ordinary doubling and one addition give \(3D=(21+17d,18)\). The secant formula at \(3D\equiv-D\bmod5\) gives the identity formal parameter
\[
 \left.-X/Y\right|_{4D}\equiv
       \frac{X(3D)-X(D)}{Y(3D)-Y(D)}\equiv 5d\pmod{25}.
\]
The denominator is a unit. In the formal logarithm for \(dX/(2Y)\) one therefore has \(\log(4D)/5\equiv d\bmod5\). This also certifies \(D\) is nontorsion. The rank bound implies the logarithm of every global point in the formal group is a rational multiple of \(\log(4D)\): multiply a global relation modulo torsion to kill the torsion, then take formal logarithms. Consequently every nonzero such logarithm, divided by five to its exact valuation, reduces to \(\mathbf F_5^\times d\), regardless of any saturation index. [Finite arithmetic verifier](abc_full_quartic_square_tangent.py)

For each rational \(r\equiv1\bmod5\) lifting to \(C(K)\), its ordinate reduces to one of \(\pm4\). Let \(R_*\) be the point with that sign, so its difference from the lifted point is formal. These are smooth residue disks with coordinate \(s\), since the two ordinates at the seed are units. If \(r\ne1\), put \(k=v_5(s)\ge1\). Integrating the differential \(-ds/(2y_c)\) on that disk gives
\[
       \log(P_r-R_*)\equiv -s/(2y_c(R_*))\pmod{5^{k+1}}.
\]
Indeed \(1/y_c(s)\) is an integral power series; for terms of integrated degree \(j\ge2\) the valuation is at least \(jk-v_5(j)\ge k+1\). The leading coefficient after dividing by \(5^k\) is a nonzero element of \(\mathbf F_5\), disjoint from \(\mathbf F_5^\times d\). This contradiction leaves just \(r=1\). Then \(X(nP)=X(P)\) and nontorsion of \(P\) gives \(n=\pm1\), proving the assertion.

## A terminating bound for each squarefree-kernel cutoff

Given an integer \(Y\ge2\), one can now compute exactly all positive odd \(n\) with \(P^+(\operatorname{sf}(w_n))\le Y\), taking the largest prime of 1 to be 1. If \(n>1\) let \(q=P^+(n)\). The theorem proves \(w_q\) is nonsquare, so choose any odd-exponent prime \(p\mid w_q\). It is good, has exact order \(q\), and satisfies \(p\ge(2\sqrt q-1)^2>q\) by the full rational 2-torsion and Hasse. Since \(p\nmid n\), its exponent stays odd in \(w_n\) by the good formal-group valuation formula. Thus a possible \(n\) has \(P^+(n)<Y\).

For every odd prime \(\ell<Y\), compute successively \(w_{\ell^e}\) from \(e=1\), strip the finitely many primes up to \(Y\), and stop at the first nonsquare residual. This procedure terminates: infinitely many square residuals along a fixed prime-power sequence would give infinitely many distinct slopes on one of the finitely many fixed genus-two curves \(v^2=-2d(X^2+3)F_0(X,1)\) for squarefree \(d\) supported at primes up to \(Y\), contradicting Faltings. At the stopping exponent \(e_\ell\) there is an odd-valuation prime \(p>Y\) in \(w_{\ell^{e_\ell}}\), persisting at every multiple with all index primes below \(Y\). Consequently the desired \(n\) are among the divisors of \(\prod_{\ell<Y,\ \ell\ {\rm odd\ prime}}\ell^{e_\ell-1}\). Exact evaluation and stripping on this finite list gives the complete answer. This computes the final exceptional index for each prescribed cutoff; termination uses qualitative finiteness and supplies no running-time estimate.
