# Rational cubes represented by the ninth and eighteenth cyclotomic forms

The only rational projective slopes \([A:B]\) with
\(\Phi_9(A,B)=A^6+A^3B^3+B^6=z^3\) are
\([0:1],[-1:1],[1:0]\). For \(\Phi_{18}(A,B)=\Phi_9(A,-B)\) they
are \([0:1],[1:1],[1:0]\). Each has a unique rational cube root in
primitive coordinates. In particular neither form is a cube for
coprime \(A>B>0\). More generally, the smooth genus-two curve
\(W^2=(s^3+1)(s^3-3)\) has exactly three rational points: \((-1,0)\)
and its two points at infinity. Consequently \(p^3\nmid\Phi_9(A,B)\) for every
prime \(p>A^2\): the positive multiplier would be less than \(3\),
and the form is odd. The proof uses two elliptic curves, one over
\(\mathbf Q\) and one over \(\mathbf Q(\sqrt[3]{3})\).

## Two simultaneous squares

For a cyclotomic cube with \(AB\ne0\), put \(x=z,\ y=AB,\ s=x/y\). Then
\[
             x^3+y^3=(A^3+B^3)^2,\qquad
             x^3-3y^3=(A^3-B^3)^2.
\]
Thus it suffices to determine the rational points of
\(W^2=(s^3+1)(s^3-3)\), with \(W=(A^6-B^6)/(A^3B^3)\) in the
cyclotomic case. For an arbitrary finite slope write \(s=x/y\) in
lowest terms. A zero factor gives just \(s=-1\). Otherwise choose the
common sign of \(x,y\) making \(x^3+y^3,\ x^3-3y^3\) positive. Their
product is an integer square and their gcd divides \(4\), so they
are both squares or both twice squares. In the latter case \(x,y\)
are odd and the difference of the two squares after dividing by two
is \(2y^3\equiv2\bmod4\), impossible. Thus both factors are squares.
In particular \(x\) is odd: for even \(x\), the odd squares would force
\(y^3\equiv-3y^3\equiv1\bmod8\).

The positive factors \(x+y,\ x^2-xy+y^2\) of the first square thus
share a squarefree kernel \(d=1\) or \(3\), since their gcd divides three.
When \(3\mid x+y\), the second factor has valuation exactly one at \(3\),
as it equals \((x+y)^2-3xy\). Also \(3\nmid x\), since otherwise
\(x^3-3y^3\) would have valuation one. Its square condition gives
\(x\equiv1\bmod3\).

Multiplying the two displayed squares gives a rational square
\((s^3+1)(s^3-3)\). Since \((s^2-s+1)/d\) is also a rational square,
we get \(v^2=d(s+1)(s^3-3)\). For \(s\ne-1\), set
\(U=-4d/(s+1),\ V=-4dv/(s+1)^2\). The equation becomes
\(V^2=U^3+3dU^2+12d^2U+16d^3\). At \(d=3\), translating to \(X=U+3\)
gives \(E_0\colon V^2=X^3+81X+162\), whose only rational point is
infinity by the descent below. Hence \(d=1\), and the square \(x+y\)
is prime to three by the previous valuation calculation. It follows that
\(x+y\equiv1\bmod3\), so \(3\mid y\).

If \(y\) were odd, the square \(x^2-xy+y^2\) would force \(xy\equiv1
\bmod8\), hence \(x\equiv y\bmod8\). Then \(x+y\equiv2\) or \(6\bmod8\)
could not be square. For even \(y\), the same square forces
\(y(y-x)\equiv0\bmod8\), so \(8\mid y\). Now \(x^3-3y^3\) square implies
\(x\equiv1\bmod8\).

## The pure cubic field

Let \(r^3=3,\ K=\mathbf Q(r)\), with \(r>0\) at its real embedding.
The ring of integers is \(\mathbf Z[r]\) of discriminant \(-243\).
The power basis is maximal away from \(3\) by its discriminant, and at
\(3\) by the Eisenstein polynomial of the uniformizer \(r\).
The Minkowski bound \(8\sqrt{243}/(9\pi)<4.42\) suffices for class
number one. The prime ideals of norms \(2,4,3\) are generated by
\(r-1,\ \sigma=r^2+r+1,\ r\), respectively, from the factorization
modulo \(2\) and the identity \((r-1)\sigma=2\). Thus all ideals of
norm up to four are principal. The positive unit
\(\epsilon=r^2-2\) has norm one. In the degree-one completion at \(2\),
\(r\equiv3\bmod8\), so \(\epsilon\equiv7\bmod8\) is a nonsquare.
Dirichlet's theorem for signature \((1,1)\) gives precisely the
unit squareclasses \(1,-1,\epsilon,-\epsilon\).

The factors \(x-ry,\ x^2+rxy+r^2y^2\) of \(x^3-3y^3\) are coprime
as ideals: a common prime cannot divide \(y\) and must divide \(3r^2\)
by substitution, but \(3\mid y,\ 3\nmid x\). Both are positive at the
real place. They have square ideals, and \(x-ry\equiv1\bmod8\) at
the degree-one dyadic place excludes its nonsquare unit class.
Hence both factors are squares in \(K\). Combining the quadratic
factor with the rational square \(x^2-xy+y^2\) gives a point on
\[
            V^2=(s^2-s+1)(s^2+rs+r^2).
\]

Write the quartic as \(s^4+a s^3+b s^2+c s+e\). Put
\[
 U=2V+2s^2+as,\quad L=4U+4b-a^2,\quad M=aU+2c,\quad
 W=(Ls+M)/2.
\]
Expanding gives the elliptic curve over \(K\)
\[
  E\colon W^2=(U+D_1)(U+D_2)(U+D_3),\qquad
    (D_1,D_2,D_3)=(1+r^2,r,-2r).
\]
Indeed the quartic gives \(Ls^2+2Ms=U^2-4e\) and
\(4W^2=L(U^2-4e)+M^2\).

## The rational auxiliary curve

For \(E_0\), \(X^3+81X+162\) has the root
\(\lambda=3r-3r^2\), which generates \(K\). Write
\(X=u/v^2,\ V=j/v^3\) with \(\gcd(u,v)=1\). Then
\(\alpha=u-\lambda v^2\) has square norm \(j^2\).
Away from \(2,3\) the discriminant \(-16\cdot3^{11}\) of the cubic is
a unit. A prime divisor of \(\alpha\) must have residue degree one and
is the unique prime above its rational prime with
\(\lambda\equiv u/v^2\). Thus its valuation is even. At \(3\) the
unique prime has degree one, so its valuation is even. At \(2\) the
degree-one prime likewise has even valuation by the norm parity.
Class number one and positivity at the real place give
\[
           \alpha=C\gamma^2,\qquad
           C\in\{1,\epsilon,\sigma,\epsilon\sigma\},\quad
           \gamma\in\mathcal O_K .
\]
Here \(\sigma\) removes a valuation only when it is positive and odd.
For these four choices the numbers of pairs
\((u,v)\bmod8\), not both even, compatible with the equation mod \(8\)
are \(8,0,0,0\). The check enumerates \(\gamma\bmod4\) in the power
basis, since this determines \(\gamma^2\bmod8\).
Therefore \(X-\lambda\) is a square in \(K\) for every rational point.
The usual tangent-line halving argument makes doubling surjective on
\(E_0(\mathbf Q)\), so its rank is zero. At the good primes \(7,13\)
the reduction orders are \(5,13\), respectively, forcing trivial
rational torsion by prime-to-residue-characteristic injection.

## Rank over \(K\)

Full two-descent uses the two squareclasses of \(U+D_1,U+D_2\), with a
vanishing factor at two-torsion replaced by the product of the other
two factors. This is the Kummer injection on \(E(K)/2E(K)\). The
norms of \(D_1-D_2,D_1-D_3,D_2-D_3\) are \(16,16,81\). Away from
\(2,3\), integral \(U\) therefore makes at most one coordinate
\(U+D_i\) a nonunit, of even valuation by the equation. For
nonintegral \(U\), all three valuations equal the even valuation of
\(U\). The torsion formulas give the same prime support. Thus each
coordinate is represented by a subset product of
\(-1,\epsilon,r-1,\sigma,r\). At the real place \(D_1\) is largest,
so the first coordinate is positive.

The following local abscissae generate the local Kummer images.
All triples use the basis \(1,r,r^2\). The verifier checks that each
nontorsion abscissa lifts locally and computes the size of each
generated subgroup.

| Place | Abscissae in addition to \(-D_1\) | Image order |
|---|---|---:|
| Above \(3\) | \((0,-2,-2)\) | 4 |
| Degree one above \(2\) | \((-2,-2,2),(-2,-1,-1)\) | 8 |
| Degree two above \(2\) | \((-2,-1,0),(-2,0,-1),(-2,1,1)\) | 16 |

These orders exhaust the local images. For a sufficiently small
torsion-free open subgroup \(H\) identified with an additive lattice
by the formal logarithm,
\([E(K_v):2E(K_v)]=\#E[2](K_v)[H:2H]\). The first factor is four.
At \(3\), valuation parity and the unit residue modulo \(r\) determine
a squareclass. At each dyadic completion use valuation parity and its
unit squareclass modulo eight, which suffices by Hensel's lemma.
The two completions send \(r\) to \(r_0\) and \(r_0\omega\), where
\(r_0\in\mathbf Z_2^\times,\ r_0^3=3\) and \(\omega^2+\omega+1=0\).

The \(1024\) global pairs leave \(512\) after the real signature, \(128\)
after the place above \(3\), \(32\) after the degree-one dyadic place,
and \(8\) after the other dyadic place. Thus the global rank is at most
one, since all four points of \(E[2]\) are defined over \(K\).

## A ramified \(3\)-adic disk

Take \(P=(-2r,2r-2r^2)\in E(K)\), and write \(\tau=-U/W\) for the
parameter at infinity. Exact group arithmetic at \(24P\) gives
\[
      v_r(U(24P))=-6,\qquad
      \tau(24P)/3\equiv 2+2r^2\pmod{3\mathcal O_{K_3}},
\]
where \(v_r(r)=1\). Reduction in this congruence is in the full ring
\(\mathbf F_3[r]/(r^3)\). The nonzero \(r^2\) coefficient is essential.

For the proposed point, \(q=1/s=y/x\in3\mathbf Z_3\) and \(q\ne0\).
Choose the sign of \(V\) so that \(Vq^2\) is congruent to \(1\) modulo
the maximal ideal. Its square is \(1+a q+b q^2+c q^3+e q^4\), hence
\(Vq^2=1+O(q)\) with integral coefficients, by Hensel's lemma at
the odd prime. The change of variables gives
\(Uq^2=4+O(q)\) and \(Wq^3=8+O(q)\). This point is in the formal
neighborhood of infinity with \(\tau/q=-1/2+O(q)\).

The formal logarithm has \(\log_E(\tau)/\tau\equiv1\bmod3\mathcal O_{K_3}\)
for \(\tau\in3\mathcal O_{K_3}\). Indeed its degree-\(j\) coefficient
lies in \(j^{-1}\mathcal O_{K_3}\), and \(j-1-v_3(j)\ge1\) for
\(j\ge2\). The logarithm is injective on this neighborhood.
In particular \(24P\) is nontorsion, so the rank is one. The
logarithm of every nonzero global point in this neighborhood must be
a nonzero rational multiple of \(\log_E(24P)\), by finite generation
and the rank bound. Its first nonzero residue after dividing by a
rational power of \(3\) is therefore proportional over \(\mathbf F_3\)
to \(2+2r^2\). On the other hand the proposed point has
\(\log_E(\tau)/q\equiv-1/2\bmod3\mathcal O_{K_3}\), and \(q\) is
rational. Its first residue belongs to \(\mathbf F_3^\times\).
This contradiction leaves only \((-1,0)\) and the two rational points at
infinity on the monic genus-two model. For \(AB\ne0\), \(s=-1\) forces
\(A^3+B^3=0\), so \(A=-B\). Finally \(AB=0\) and \(A=-B\) give the
claimed cubes directly.

## The cofactor three and all the lifted orders

The only rational projective slope with \(\Phi_9(A,B)=3z^3\) is \([1:1]\). The corresponding slope at order 18 is \([-1:1]\). For \(B\ne0\), put \(t=(A/B)^3,\ w=z/B^2\). Then \(t^2+t+1=3w^3\), and
\[
        (t+2)^3+(1-t)^3=9(t^2+t+1)=(3w)^3 .
\]
Fermat's theorem for exponent three forces \(t=-2\) or \(1\). Since \(-2\) is not a rational cube, only \(A=B\) remains, and it works. At \(B=0\), the putative cube would have 3-adic valuation incongruent to that of \(A^6\). Replacing \(B\) by \(-B\) proves the order-18 assertion. Also \(v_3(\Phi_9(A,B))\) and \(v_3(\Phi_{18}(A,B))\) are each zero or one at coprime bases: if \(3\) divides the value, the two inputs \(A^3,\pm B^3\) are equal nonzero residues modulo three, and their sum of three quadratic monomials has valuation one by the odd-prime lifting formula (or reduction modulo nine).

Thus for every \(m=2^i3^j\) with \(j\ge2\), every coprime \(A>B>0\) has a prime of exact order \(m\) whose multiplicity is **not divisible by three**. Indeed \(\Phi_m(A,B)=\Phi_d(A^u,B^u)\) with \(d=9\) or \(18\) and \(u=m/d\). The value is odd and its only possible factor at 3 has exponent one. If all other valuations were multiples of three, it would be a cube or three times a cube, both excluded here. The odd-multiplicity result supplies a possibly different prime at the same order. At any of these orders, \(p>A^{2u}\) also implies \(p^3\nmid\Phi_m(A,B)\), by applying the ninth-order cube exclusion or the strict size bound at order 18 to the powered inputs. [Order substitutions and parity theorem](abc_cyclotomic_odd_primitive_grid.md)

For the entire differences \(A^9-B^9\) and \(A^{18}-B^{18}\), every prime \(p>A^2\) has valuation at most two. Such a prime divides neither index and can divide at most one cyclotomic factor. The factors at orders \(1,2,3,6\) are at most \(3A^2<A^6<p^3\); at orders \(9,18\) use the results above.

## The rational involution at the seventh index

For \(C\colon z^3=\Phi_7(x)\), the involution
\((x,z)\mapsto(1/x,z/x^2)\) gives the quotient
\[
           u^3=v^3+v^2-2v-1,\qquad v=x+x^{-1},\quad u=z/x.
\]
This plane cubic has a rational point at infinity. PARI/GP's
\(\mathrm{ellfromeqn}\) gives the Jacobian \(Y^2-7Y=X^3\), and
\(\mathrm{ellrank}\) gives rank interval \([1,1]\).

This is the only rational degree-two quotient of \(C\), up to isomorphism.
The four regular differentials \(dx/z,\ dx/z^2,\ x\,dx/z^2,\ x^2dx/z^2\)
are independent, and Riemann–Hurwitz gives genus four. Their canonical
map \([z:1:x:x^2]\) is birational onto its image; the image has degree
six and lies on the quadric cone \(X_1X_3=X_2^2\) in coordinates
\([X_0:X_1:X_2:X_3]\). This quadric is unique by Bézout. Any rational
automorphism therefore preserves its ruling and descends to a rational
Möbius map preserving the six roots of \(\Phi_7\), the branch locus
of the ruling map \(x\).

Writing a primitive seventh root as \(\zeta\), such a map gives
\(\alpha\zeta+\beta=(\gamma\zeta+\delta)\zeta^k\) for some
\(1\le k\le6\). For \(2\le k\le4\) this is a polynomial relation of
degree below six, and for \(k=5\) it is missing coefficients of the
minimal polynomial \(\Phi_7\); both alternatives force a singular
matrix. For \(k=1,6\) the relation gives the identity or inversion.
There is no nonidentity rational automorphism over the \(x\)-line,
since it would multiply \(z\) by a primitive cube root of unity and the
constant field is \(\mathbf Q\). Thus inversion is the only rational
involution. The seventh-index cube classification remains open in this
argument; the uniqueness calculation concerns degree-two quotients.
