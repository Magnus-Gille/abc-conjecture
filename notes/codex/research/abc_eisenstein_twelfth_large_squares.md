# Large square divisors and abc hits on the twelfth-power Eisenstein conic

The number of primitive positive triples \(U^2+3V^2=W^{12}\), \(C=W^{12}\le X\), satisfying \(C\ge\operatorname{rad}(3UVW)\) is
\[
                           O(X^{1/18+o(1)}).
\]
This gives a power saving also relative to the population with every prime divisor of \(W\) equal to \(1\bmod24\). There are \(\gg X^{1/12}/\log X\) members of that population already with \(W\) prime. The condition \(24\mid V\) in fact holds for every primitive positive solution. Finiteness of the subfamily with \(C\ge \operatorname{rad}(3UVW)^{1+\epsilon}\) remains open. [Equivalence of the restricted family with rational abc](abc_eisenstein_power_conic.md)

## Parameters and the reduced binary form

Primitivity forces \(U,W\) odd, \(V\) even, and \(3\nmid UW\). Indeed \(U\) even and \(V\) odd would give \(3\bmod4\); both odd would give 2-adic valuation two. The Eisenstein ideals generated by \(U\pm V\sqrt{-3}\) are coprime: any common prime ideal dividing their norm is away from 2 and 3 and would divide both \(U,V\). Their product is a twelfth power and the ring is principal, so \(U+V\sqrt{-3}=\zeta\beta^{12}\) for a unit \(\zeta\). Modulo 2, the nonzero residue group has order three, and \(\zeta\equiv1\) forces \(\zeta=\pm1\). Multiplying \(\beta\) by a sixth root of unity, choose \(\beta\equiv1\bmod2\mathcal O\), so \(\beta=x+y\sqrt{-3}\) with integral \(x,y\). Its norm is \(W=x^2+3y^2\); coprimality with its conjugate implies \((x,y)=1\). Such pairs have \(H=\max(|x|,|y|)\asymp C^{1/24}\).

Write \(\beta^{12}=P+Q\sqrt{-3}\). Direct expansion gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
 F_0&=x^4-18x^2y^2+9y^4,\qquad
 F_\pm=F_0\pm(12x^3y-36xy^3),\\
 P&=F_0F_+F_-,\\
 Q&=12xy(x-y)(x+y)(x-3y)(x+3y)
          (x^2-3y^2)(x^2-6xy-3y^2)(x^2+6xy-3y^2).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since \(W\) is odd, \(xy\) is even, proving \(24\mid V=|Q|\). The integral form \(G= P (Q/12) W\) has degree 26. It has 26 distinct projective roots: the Möbius coordinate \(\beta/\bar\beta\) sends those of \(P Q\) bijectively to the 24th roots of unity, and those of \(W\) to zero and infinity. In particular all its irreducible factors have degree at most four. For a solution, \(G(x,y)\ne0\) and \(\operatorname{rad}|G(x,y)|\le\operatorname{rad}(3UVW)\).

## Counting square classes of the quartics

Uniformly for squarefree signed \(b\), the number of primitive pairs of height at most \(H\) satisfying \(F_i(x,y)=bz^2\ne0\), for any one of the three quartics, is
\[
                  \exp(O(\log(3H)/\log\log(3H)))= H^{o(1)} .
                  \tag{1}
\]
For \(F_0\), set \(r=x/y\) and \(v=z/y^2\), omitting the boundedly many primitive pairs on the axes. The relation gives a point
\[
 (X,Y)=(b r^2,b^2rv)\quad\text{on}\quad
 E_b:\quad Y^2=X^3-18bX^2+9b^2 X .
\]
There are only boundedly many primitive pairs for each point, by its \(X\) coordinate. The other quartics are fixed linear changes of \(F_0\) up to rational constants. Indeed for \(\omega=(-1+\sqrt{-3})/2\), the real parts of \((\omega\beta)^4\) and \((\bar\omega\beta)^4\) are \(-F_\pm/2\). Explicitly the two maps are \(M_+(x,y)=(-x-3y,x-y)\) and \(M_-(x,y)=(-x+3y,-x-y)\), and \(F_0(M_\pm(x,y))=-8F_\pm(x,y)\). Their determinants equal 4, so for primitive input the new coordinate gcd divides 4. After primitive reduction the signed squarefree part is the squarefree part of \(-8b\). These fixed fractional linear transformations are bijective on projective rational points and change height by at most a constant factor.

For completeness, the rational 2-isogeny descent on \(E_b\) gives
\[
 r_b=\operatorname{rank} E_b(\mathbb Q)\le 2\omega(|b|)+O(1).
\]
In the standard descent for \(Y^2=X^3+aX^2+cX\), the image of the squareclass map is supported on the primes dividing \(c\), together with the sign; the 2-isogenous curve has last coefficient \(a^2-4c\). Here the two coefficients are \(9b^2,288b^2\). More precisely, the squareclass map sends an ordinary point to \([X]\), the identity to 1 and \((0,0)\) to \([c]\). For a prime away from \(c\), if \(v(X)\) is positive then \(v(Y^2)=v(X)\), while if it is negative then \(v(Y^2)=3v(X)\); in either case it is even. The kernels of the two squareclass maps are the images of the dual isogenies. Since the composition of the isogenies is multiplication by two, \(E_b(\mathbb Q)/2E_b(\mathbb Q)\) has size bounded above by the product of the two squareclass image sizes. The first size is at most \(2^{1+\omega(3|b|)}\), the second at most \(2^{1+\omega(6|b|)}\). This proves the rank bound including any rational torsion. [Classical 2-isogeny descent, Chapter X of Silverman, *The Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves*](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-0-387-09494-6)

Over \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt b)\) the curve is isomorphic to the fixed \(E_1\), with abscissa \(X/b\). Hence \(\hat h(T)=\tfrac12 h(X(T)/b)+O(1)\) for its nonidentity points, with an absolute constant. A nontorsion point has \(Y(T)\ne0\); writing \(X(T)/b=s/t\) in lowest terms, \(b\) is the squareclass of \(st(s^2-18st+9t^2)\). Thus \(\log|b|\le4h(s/t)+O(1)\). It follows that the minimum positive canonical height is \(\gg\log(2|b|)\), using the fixed-field Mordell–Weil lattice for the finitely many small \(b\). The torsion sizes are uniformly bounded, also by bounded \(h(X/b)\) and rationality of \(X/b\). All the points constructed above have \(\hat h\le\log H+O(1)\).

Packing Euclidean balls in the Mordell–Weil lattice now bounds the count by
\[
 O(1)\left(1+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(2H)}{\log(2|b|)}}\right)\right)^{2\omega(|b|)+O(1)} .
\]
Only \(|b|\ll H^4\) occur. Put \(L=\log(3H)\), \(t=\log(2|b|)\). For \(t\le\sqrt L\) the displayed bound has logarithm \(O(\sqrt L\log L)\). For larger \(t\) use \(\omega(|b|)\ll t/\log(2t)\) and \(t\log(2+\sqrt{L/t})\ll L\) on \(t=O(L)\). This proves (1).

In particular, on any projective angular interval of width \(\Delta\le1\) about a real root of a given quartic \(F_i\), the number of primitive pairs with **any** \(q\ge Z\) satisfying \(q^2\mid F_i(x,y)\ne0\) is
\[
                    \ll (1+H^4\Delta/Z^2)H^{o(1)}.             \tag{2}
\]
The size of \(|F_i|\) there is \(O(H^4\Delta)\) by the mean-value theorem on the unit circle. Its signed squarefree part is at most \(|F_i|/q^2\). Use \(\Delta=1\) and the ordinary global size bound when no interval is prescribed. For prime powers \(p^e\mid F_i\) with \(p\ge Z\) one may replace \(Z^2\) in (2) by \(Z^{2\lfloor e/2\rfloor}\).

## Congruence spacing and the complete radical

For an integer \(q\ge1\) there are at most \(O_G(26^{\omega(q)})\) projective root classes of \(G\bmod q^2\). This is Hensel lifting and CRT at the good primes. At each of the finitely many bad primes \(p\), the number of classes is uniformly bounded over all depths: work in either integral projective chart, use separability and a Bézout identity with the derivative to bound its valuation at high-precision zeros, then use Hensel's lemma past twice that fixed valuation. At a finite \(p\)-adic zero the number of residue classes at sufficiently high precision is at most \(p^{v_p(G')}\), using the local affine form in the chart; finite low levels can change only the constant.

Two distinct rational directions reducing to the same projective class modulo \(q^2\) have determinant a nonzero multiple of \(q^2\). For parameters of height at most \(2H\), their angular separation in the projective circle is therefore \(\gg q^2/H^2\) whenever two such directions exist. Each direction has at most two primitive representatives. In an interval of width \(\Delta\) there are \(O(1+\Delta H^2/q^2)\) per class. In particular the number of pairs with a prime \(p\in[Z,R]\) squaring into a given quartic in such an interval is \(\ll R/\log(2R)+\Delta H^2/(Z\log(2Z))\) for \(R\ge Z\ge2\). For a strip around its own real root, combining this with (2) at \(Z=R\) bounds the entire prime tail, including arbitrarily large primes.

Count the cases \(H\le\max(|x|,|y|)\le2H\). The number within \(H^{-2/3}\) of any real projective zero of \(G\) is \(O(H^{4/3})\) by the same spacing at modulus one. Off those intervals, distinctness of the geometric zeros gives \(|G(x,y)|\gg H^{26-2/3}\), since only one normalized linear factor can approach zero at a time. If \(C\ge\operatorname{rad}(3UVW)\), then
\[
           |G|/\operatorname{rad}|G|\gg H^{26-2/3}/H^{24}=H^{4/3}.
\]
Writing \(q_*=\prod_{p\mid G}p^{\lfloor v_p(G)/2\rfloor}\), one has \(q_*^2\ge |G|/\operatorname{rad}|G|\); hence \(q_*\gg H^{2/3}\).

If every prime divisor of \(q_*\) is at most \(H^{4/3}\), then \(q_*\) has a divisor \(q\) between \(cH^{2/3}\) and \(H^{4/3}\) for a sufficiently small fixed \(c>0\). Use a prime in that interval if available; otherwise accumulate factors no larger than \(cH^{2/3}\) until first passing its lower endpoint. Summing the root count and congruence spacing over this range gives
\[
     \sum_{cH^{2/3}\le q\le H^{4/3}} O_G(26^{\omega(q)})
                      (1+H^2/q^2)
       \ll H^{4/3+o(1)} .
\]
Otherwise \(p^2\mid G\) for a prime \(p>H^{4/3}\). Once \(H\) is large such a prime avoids all the fixed pairwise resultants, so its square divides one factor. The linear and quadratic factors are too small. For each of the three quartics, (2) with no angular restriction bounds these exceptions by \(H^{4/3+o(1)}\). Summing the dyadic intervals proves the stated bound in \(C\).

More generally, for fixed \(0\le\eta<2\), the number in the same shell satisfying \(C/\operatorname{rad}(3UVW)\ge H^{-\eta}\) is
\[
                         O(H^{(4+\eta)/3+o(1)}).
\]
Put \(a=(2-\eta)/3\). The angular neighborhoods of width \(H^{-a}\) contain \(O(H^{2-a})\) primitive pairs. Else \(|G|/\operatorname{rad}|G|\gg H^{2-\eta-a}=H^{2a}\), so \(q_*\gg H^a\). If \(q_*\) has no prime larger than \(H^{4/3}\), it has a divisor between \(cH^a\) and \(H^{4/3}\), since \(2a\le4/3\). The congruence sum is now \(O(H^{4/3+o(1)}+H^{2-a+o(1)})\); the extreme prime bound remains \(H^{4/3+o(1)}\). This proves the formula, in particular \(O(H^{5/3+o(1)})\) when \(\eta=1\).

Finally each prime \(p\equiv1\bmod24\) splits in the Eisenstein ring. A generator of one prime ideal can be made \(1\bmod2\) by a unit, giving integral coordinates and norm \(p\). Its twelfth power has both coordinates nonzero, since its principal ideal is coprime and unequal to its conjugate. This produces one primitive triple with \(W=p\), and its \(24\)-divisibility was shown above. Distinct primes give distinct heights. The prime number theorem in this progression proves the claimed population lower bound. The count is an absolute estimate for all the primitive solutions and does not presuppose distribution of a more narrowly parametrized transfer sequence.

## An extreme-prime bound in the degree-sixteen transfer factors

In the transfer with \(\beta=s^4+\omega t^4\), write \(h=\max(|s|,|t|)\) for primitive input. Up to fixed scalars the three numerator factors are the degree-sixteen forms \(B_i(s,t)=F_i(2s^4-t^4,t^4)\). Each has simple geometric zeros: the projective fourth-power map branches only on the axes and their images have nonzero \(F_i\). For each fixed squareclass, the number of primitive inputs of height at most \(h\) with \(B_i\) in that class is \(h^{o(1)}\). Indeed the pair \((2s^4-t^4,t^4)\) has height \(O(h^4)\) and coordinate gcd dividing 2. Its primitive reduction preserves the squareclass of a quartic value. The fixed map from input directions to that primitive direction has degree four, so (1) applies with bounded multiplicity.

Accordingly the number with \(q^2\mid B_i\ne0\) for some \(q\ge Z\) is
\[
              O\big((1+h^{16}\Delta/Z^2)h^{o(1)}\big)
\]
in a projective angular interval of width \(\Delta\) about a real zero, and with \(\Delta=1\) over all inputs. In particular, for \(0<\delta<1\) the number with a prime square \(p^2\mid B_i\), \(p>h^{7+\delta}\), is \(O(h^{2-2\delta+o(1)})\). The intermediate-prime range at this more restrictive input scale is larger than the one handled by the degree-four square-class count on the unrestricted parametrization.
