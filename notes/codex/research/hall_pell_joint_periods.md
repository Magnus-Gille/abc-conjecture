# Common periods at a single Pell index

Let $n=7+12j$, $j\ge0$, and put $G_n=(L_n-6)^2-20$, $H_n=(L_n-9)^2-5$, $C_n=G_nH_n$ and $M_n=(L_n-11)F_nC_n$. For an integer $D\ge1$ coprime to $30$ let $T(D)$ be the least $h\ge1$ with $D\mid F_{6h}$. The radical saving supplied by the quadratics is $C_n/\operatorname{rad}(C_n)$. This integer is coprime to $30$, since $C_n$ is coprime to $6$ and $v_5(C_n)=1$. [Construction](hall_pell_direction.md)

**Common-period bound.** Uniformly over all indices on the progression,

$$\sup_n \log\gcd(C_n,F_{6h})=o(\sqrt h)\qquad(h\longrightarrow\infty). \tag{1}$$

In particular, fix $\delta>0$. Along any sequence with $n\to\infty$, $D_n\mid C_n$, $\gcd(D_n,30)=1$ and $\log D_n\ge\delta n$, one must have

$$\frac{T(D_n)}{n^2}\longrightarrow\infty. \tag{2}$$

The number $D_n$ can combine arbitrarily many prime powers. If $D_n$ is the product of one $p$ for each chosen $p^2\mid C_n$, (2) already applies to the common period modulo those primes. The estimates obtained from the Subspace Theorem here are qualitative.

## An order bound for two independent algebraic units

Fix a number field $E$ and multiplicatively independent algebraic units $u,v\in E$. For any nonzero proper integral ideal $I$ of its ring of integers, reduce the two units modulo $I$. Then

$$\sup_{|\langle u,v\bmod I\rangle|\le H}\log N(I)=o(\sqrt H), \tag{3}$$

with the supremum taken over ideals satisfying the indicated bound.

We use the following consequence of Corvaja and Zannier's height theorem. Let $\Gamma$ be a fixed finitely generated group of pairs of algebraic units of $E$. For every $\eta>0$, the pairs $(x,y)\in\Gamma$ for which the normalized logarithmic gcd

$$g_E(x,y)=\frac{\log N((x-1,y-1))}{[E:\mathbb Q]}$$

exceeds $\eta\max\{h(x),h(y)\}$ lie in a finite union of translates of proper subtori, with isolated exceptional points allowed. Here $(x-1,y-1)$ is the ideal generated by the two elements and $h$ is absolute logarithmic height. For these units the finite-place gcd is bounded above by $h(1:x-1:y-1)-h(x-1:y-1)$. Apply the rational-function height theorem with polynomials $X-1,Y-1$, then use Laurent's theorem on intersections of finitely generated subgroups with closed subvarieties of a torus. Each exceptional translate or point satisfies an equation $x^k y^\ell=c$ with fixed $(k,\ell)\ne(0,0)$ and fixed $c$. The point $(1,1)$ can be excluded throughout. [Corvaja–Zannier, *A lower bound for the height of a rational function at S-unit points*](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0311030)

For (3) consider the relation lattice

$$\Lambda_I=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb Z^2:u^av^b\equiv1\bmod I\}.$$

Its index in $\mathbb Z^2$ is the order of the generated group. For every nonzero vector $w=(a,b)$ in this lattice,

$$\log N(I)\le \log|N_{E/\mathbb Q}(u^av^b-1)|\ll_{u,v,E}\|w\|.$$

The norm is nonzero by independence. The upper bound follows by taking the product over embeddings, using that $u,v$ and their inverses are integral.

Suppose (3) fails. For some $\epsilon>0$ there are $H_i\to\infty$ and ideals $I_i$ with lattice index at most $H_i$ and $\log N(I_i)\ge\epsilon\sqrt{H_i}$. By the norm bound every nonzero lattice vector has length $\gg_\epsilon\sqrt{H_i}$. A shortest vector $w_1$ also has length $O(\sqrt{H_i})$, by pigeonholing integer pairs in a square modulo the lattice. Extend $w_1$, primitive in the lattice, to a basis $w_1,w_2$. Subtract an integral multiple of $w_1$ from $w_2$ to make its parallel component have length at most $\|w_1\|/2$. The perpendicular component has length $[\mathbb Z^2:\Lambda_{I_i}]/\|w_1\|\ll_\epsilon\sqrt{H_i}$. Both basis vectors are therefore $O_\epsilon(\sqrt{H_i})$.

Let $x_i,y_i$ be the units formed from $u,v$ using these two exponent vectors. Their heights are $O_\epsilon(\sqrt{H_i})$. Both reduce to one modulo $I_i$, so $g_E(x_i,y_i)\ge\log N(I_i)/[E:\mathbb Q]\gg_\epsilon\sqrt{H_i}$. For a sufficiently small fixed $\eta>0$ the gcd theorem puts an infinite subsequence on one exceptional equation. Since $u,v$ are multiplicatively independent, $x_i^k y_i^\ell=c$ means that $kw_1+\ell w_2$ is one *fixed* exponent vector. It is nonzero by linear independence of the basis and belongs to each corresponding lattice. This contradicts the lower bound on the length of its shortest vector. This proves (3).

## Applying the order bound to the quadratics

Set $\alpha=(1+\sqrt5)/2$ and $q=\alpha^{12}$. For $p\ge7$ the order of $q$ modulo $p^e$ is the least $h$ such that $p^e\mid F_{6h}$, since $q^h-1=\sqrt5\,\alpha^{6h}F_{6h}$. Thus $T(D)$ is the least common multiple of these orders over the exact prime powers dividing $D$.

The eight roots $\beta$ needed here satisfy $\beta-\beta^{-1}=6\pm2\sqrt5$ or $9\pm\sqrt5$. They are algebraic units. Take a fixed field $E$ containing $\alpha$ and all eight roots. The pair $q,\gamma_\beta=\beta\alpha^{-7}$ is multiplicatively independent for each $\beta$. Indeed the involution over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$ in the quartic root field sends $\beta$ to $-1/\beta$, and $\beta$ is real of absolute value different from one. A relation for $q,\gamma_\beta$ would force the exponent of $\beta$ to vanish and then the exponent of $q$ to vanish. [Root fields and independence](hall_pell_shared_divisors.md)

Let $D$ be the prime-to-$30$ part of $\gcd(C_n,F_{6h})$. For every prime ideal $\mathfrak p\mid D$ in $E$, assign its full power in $D\mathcal O_E$ to the root $\beta$ congruent to $\alpha^n$ modulo that power. To justify the assignment, the product of the eight linear factors at $\alpha^n$ equals $\alpha^{4n}C_n$. Their discriminant is a unit at $p\ge7$, so exactly one factor carries the positive valuation. Let $I_\beta$ be the product of the ideal powers assigned to $\beta$. [Polynomial and discriminant](hall_pell_valuation_bounds.md)

Modulo $I_\beta$ we have $\gamma_\beta=q^j$ and $q^h=1$. Thus the two reductions generate a cyclic group of order at most $h$. For each of the finitely many $\beta$, (3) gives $\log N(I_\beta)=o(\sqrt h)$ uniformly, including the empty product. Multiplying the ideals recovers $D\mathcal O_E$, so their log norms sum to $[E:\mathbb Q]\log D$. Restoring the possible factor 5 costs at most $\log5$. This proves (1). For (2), $T(D_n)$ tends to infinity because each fixed $F_{6h}$ has finitely many divisors. Apply (1) with $h=T(D_n)$ and use $\delta n\le\log D_n$.

There is also a consequence when the orders have no common small multiple. For $p\ge7$ write $r_{p,e}=T(p^e)$. At a *fixed* index $n$, summing (1) over $h\le R$ gives

$$\sum_{p\ge7}\ \sum_{\substack{1\le e\le v_p(C_n)\\r_{p,e}\le R}}\log p
  \le \sum_{h\le R}\log\gcd(C_n,F_{6h})=o(R^{3/2})$$

uniformly in $n$ as $R\to\infty$. Each copy can be charged to $h=r_{p,e}$, since a gcd containing $p^k$ pays for all $k$ copies. For $(L_n-11)F_n$ the corresponding bound is $O(R(\tau(n-5)+\tau(n)+\tau(n+5)))$ with $\tau$ the divisor-counting function. Taking $R=K n^{2/3}$ for any fixed $K>0$ now gives $o(n)$ for all four factors at every index. [Fibonacci and Lucas divisor bound](hall_pell_aggregate_bounds.md)

## A later occurrence of a large common divisor

Let $U_n$ denote either quadratic and $V_m$ any of the four factors at an index on the progression. Exclude identical entries. For every fixed $K>0$,

$$\sup_{m\le K n^2}\log\gcd(U_n,V_m)=o(n)\qquad(n\longrightarrow\infty).$$

Otherwise choose a subsequence with log gcd at least $\delta n$. Enlarge the fixed number field to contain all the roots, including the two roots for the Fibonacci factor. At places over rational primes $p\ge7$, the simple-root factorization assigns the common divisor ideal to finitely many root pairs $\beta,\xi$, with $\alpha^n\equiv\beta$ and $\alpha^m\equiv\xi$ modulo each assigned ideal. At the other primes the first quadratic contributes at most $\log5$. Some fixed pair's ideal has log norm $\gg n$ on a subsequence.

Write $m=kn+s$ with $0\le s<n$ and $0\le k\le Kn$. The units $x=\alpha^n/\beta$ and $y=\alpha^s\beta^k/\xi$ both reduce to one modulo the assigned ideal. They belong to a fixed finitely generated group and have heights $O_K(n)$. The gcd theorem again gives an exceptional equation $x^a y^b=c$ on a subsequence, also allowing $y=1$ as an exceptional equation itself. Consequently

$$\alpha^{an+bs}\beta^{-a+bk}=c\,\xi^b.$$

Independence of $\alpha,\beta$ fixes both exponents. If $b=0$, this fixes $n$, which is impossible. If $b\ne0$, it fixes $k$, so $m=O(n)$. The elementary bound $\log\gcd(U_n,V_m)<51+25\sqrt{\max(n,m)}$ for distinct entries then contradicts the assumed log gcd. In particular a divisor of size at least $\exp(\delta n)$ recurring in another factor can do so only at indices $m$ with $m/n^2\to\infty$ along an unbounded sequence. [Elementary bound and root polynomials](hall_pell_shared_divisors.md)

## What a nearby occurrence can contribute

There is a separate elementary restriction which applies even if different factors of the saving occur at different neighboring indices. Set $S_n=M_n/\operatorname{rad}(M_n)$. For integer $B\ge1$ put

$$B_n^*=\operatorname{lcm}\{M_{n+12d}:1\le|d|\le B,\ n+12d\ge7\}.$$

Then uniformly at every index,

$$\log\gcd(S_n,B_n^*)\le49B\log8+588(\log\alpha)B(B+1). \tag{4}$$

Put $V(X)=(X-11)(X^2+4)((X-6)^2-20)((X-9)^2-5)$, so $V(L_n)=5F_nM_n$. If $x=L_n,\ y=L_{n+12d}$ and $z=L_{12d}$, the Lucas identity gives $x^2+y^2-zxy=z^2-4$. Define the integer

$$\Delta(z)=\prod_{V(u)=0}\prod_{V(v)=0}\bigl(u^2+v^2-zuv-(z^2-4)\bigr).$$

Every common divisor of $M_n$ and $M_{n+12d}$ divides $\Delta(z)$. This follows by first taking the resultant of the conic polynomial with $V(Y)$ in $Y$, then with $V(X)$ in $X$. Modulo the common divisor the two successive polynomial combinations vanish when evaluated at the corresponding integer roots. The polynomials $V$ are monic, giving the displayed root product up to sign.

All roots of $V$ have absolute value less than 12. For $z=L_{12|d|}\ge322$, each factor in $\Delta(z)$ has absolute value strictly between zero and $2z^2$, by the triangle inequality. Hence the gcd in (4) divides $\prod_{d=1}^B|\Delta(L_{12d})|$. A maximal power of each shared prime occurs in at least one of these integers, and the same integer handles either sign of $d$. Since $L_{12d}<2\alpha^{12d}$, taking logs proves (4).

In particular if $B=o(\sqrt n)$, the part of $S_n$ already present in that whole neighborhood has logarithm $o(n)$. A prime occurring once at a neighbor pays for one extra copy at $n$. To pay for $e-1$ extra copies it must occur to depth at least $e-1$ somewhere in the neighborhood. Both (2) and (4) still permit an exponential saving supported on prime powers with very long common periods and little nearby overlap.
