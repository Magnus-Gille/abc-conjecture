# The gcd of full-annihilator exponents above the quartic cutoff

For every odd \(7\le n\le509\) and every prime \(7\le n\le1009\), let \(H_n\) be the part of the abscissa denominator square root of \([n]R\) supported on rational primes \(p>n^4\) at which \(R\bmod p\) has exact CM annihilator \((n)\). Here \(R=(-8,16)\) lies on \(y^2=x^3+16x^2+32x\). In **all 324 indices**, including 81 split prime indices, one has

\[
 H_n>1,\qquad \gcd_{p\mid H_n}v_p(H_n)=1.
\]

Thus at any of these indices either some such prime has exponent one, or there are at least two distinct such primes and at least one exponent is odd and at least three. In particular a squareful full-annihilator factor in this range would require the latter case. A pattern with exponents 2 and 3 meets the gcd test.

The earlier cutoff calculation identifies \(H_n=C_n/s_n\), where \(C_n\) is the part of the denominator of full CM annihilator and the complete integer \(s_n\) is recorded in the cutoff table. Its norm sieve removes the prime powers at precisely \(p\le n^4\). For this additional verification the signed scalar denominators are computed independently from the division polynomials. Write \(T_n=2^{-5(n^2-(n\bmod2))/4}\psi_n(R)\). The first five values are \(0,1,1,-9,-14\). In the ordinary parity-dependent division recurrences this scaling removes the factor \(\psi_2(R)=32\) from the even-index recurrence. In the odd-index recurrence it leaves a factor of 32 on the summand with all even arguments. This proves by induction that all the \(T_n\) are integral and that \(T_n\) is odd for odd \(n\). At odd primes, the integral point and good reduction give \(v_p(B_n)=v_p(\psi_n(R))\). Hence \(B_n=|T_n|\) for odd \(n\), using the oddness of \(B_n\) in this orbit. Removing the proper annihilators as in the earlier calculation gives \(C_n\). The verifier checks exact divisibility by \(s_n\) and the recorded bit length of the quotient. [Cutoff calculation and CM denominator formulas](abc_odd_rank_quartic_cutoff.md) · [Recorded small factors](abc_odd_rank_quartic_cutoff.csv)

If \(H_n=u^k\) for any integer \(k>1\), it is also a perfect power of some prime exponent. For each prime exponent \(k\) to be tested we have \(u\ge n^4+1\) since \(H_n>1\) has no smaller prime factor. If its bit length is \(b\), then \(k\lfloor\log_2(n^4+1)\rfloor\le b-1\). For every prime \(k\) up to this bound the verifier searches primes \(t\equiv1\pmod{2k}\) until \(H_n\bmod t\) is nonzero and \(H_n^{(t-1)/k}\not\equiv1\pmod t\). Fermat's theorem excludes a \(k\)-th power. In total 81,331 prime exponents were eliminated, and no case required testing beyond the fourteenth prime in its progression. A second calculation used exact rational addition on the elliptic curve and the FLINT perfect-power test on each remaining integer. It independently returned zero perfect powers in 324 rows.
