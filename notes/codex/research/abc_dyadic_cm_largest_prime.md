# A largest-prime-factor test for rational abc in an explicit doubling sequence

For \(n=2^k\), \(k\ge3\), start with \((u_8,v_8)=(31,14)\) and define
\[
 w_n=\sqrt{u_n^4+16u_n^2v_n^2+32v_n^4},\quad
 u_{2n}=|u_n^4-32v_n^4|,\quad v_{2n}=2u_nv_nw_n.
\]
Every displayed square root is an integer. Write \(P^+(w_n)\) for its largest prime factor. For the [primitive octic triples](abc_octic_full_mixed_orbit.md) with quality \(q_N=\log c_N/\log\operatorname{rad}(a_N b_N c_N)\), the following implication is unconditional:
\[
 \liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{P^+(w_{2^k})}{2^{4k}}=0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \limsup_{k\to\infty}q_{2^k-1}\ge\frac{16}{15}.
\]
There is also a fixed numerical test. If \(P^+(w_n)\le n^4/1000\) at infinitely many dyadic indices, the neighboring octic triples have quality exceeding \(21/20\) infinitely often. This follows by keeping the leading constant in the lattice sieve. An infinite sequence of such triples disproves rational abc, for example by taking any fixed exponent strictly between 1 and \(21/20\) as the proposed abc bound. In particular rational abc would imply \(P^+(w_n)\gg n^4\) eventually. No infinite subsequence satisfying either sufficient condition has been proved. The first three values have prime factorizations \(2273\), \(22151840117633\), and \(2401379840999699201\cdot100264636730315412574143047918721793\). [Numerical constant and height estimate](abc_octic_dyadic_fermat_audit.md)

## The integer recurrence and its contact in the abc triple

Use \(R=(-8,16)\) on \(E':y^2=x^3+16x^2+32x\), and let \(h=\widehat h(R)>0\). At \(P=[n/2]R\) write \(x(P)=u^2/v^2\) in lowest terms with \(u,v>0\). At \(n=8\) this is \(31^2/14^2\). The curve equation gives \(y(P)=\pm uw_n/v^3\), so the integer inside the square root is a rational square and therefore an integer square. The duplication formula \(x(2P)=((x(P)^2-32)/(2y(P)))^2\) gives the recurrence. Its numerator and denominator remain coprime: \(u,w_n\) are odd and \(v\) is even. Any odd prime dividing \(w_n\) is a unit on \(uv\); if it also divides \(u^4-32v^4\), then \(x(P)^2=32\) and \(x(P)^2+16x(P)+32=0\), hence \(x(P)=-4\), impossible at an odd prime. Coprimality with \(u,v\) follows directly. Positive definiteness gives
\[
 \log w_n=h_{\mathbb P^1}(x(P))+O(1)=\tfrac12 h n^2+O(1),
\]
where \(h_{\mathbb P^1}\) is the logarithmic projective height of the abscissa.

At every prime \(p\mid w_n\), the point \(P\) reduces to a nonzero two-torsion point different from \(J'=(0,0)\). The local parameter at that point is its ordinate, of valuation \(e=v_p(w_n)\). The reduced order of \(R\) is exactly \(n\), since \(n\) is a power of two and \([n/2]R\ne O\) modulo \(p\). The dual two-isogeny \(E'\to E:v^2=u^3-8u^2+8u\) sends \(R\) to \(A=(1,1)\). It sends both non-\(J'\) two-torsion points to \(J=(0,0)\), and is étale at odd primes. Consequently \(A\) also has order \(n\) modulo \(p\), and \([n/2]A\) has local depth \(e\) relative to \(J\). In the octic triple at \(N=n-1\) this point gives the slope \(s_N/t_N\). The slope function has a simple zero with unit leading coefficient at \(J\), so \(v_p(s_N)=e\). In fact this is the first occurrence of \(p\) in the entire triple family: at any earlier odd index \(3\le M<n-1\), the order \(n\) divides none of \(M,2M,M\pm1,M^2+2\). [Local formulas and the height of the triple](abc_octic_whole_radical_tail.md)

## Why all the prime factors lie on a sparse lattice

Put \(K=\mathbb Q(\tau)\), \(\tau^2=-2\). The annihilator ideal of \(R\) after reduction contains \((n)\) in \(\mathbb Z[\tau]\). All divisors of \((n)\) are powers of \((\tau)\). A strictly larger ideal would contain \(n/\tau\), forcing \([\tau]P=O\). But \(\ker[\tau]=\{O,J'\}\), and the reduction of \(P\) is neither. Thus the annihilator is exactly \((n)\).

Every \(p\mid w_n\) splits in \(K\). Otherwise CM supersingular reduction gives \(\#E'(\mathbb F_p)=p+1\). The non-\(J'\) rational two-torsion point already yields full rational two-torsion. Adjoining that group to the cyclic subgroup generated by \(R\) gives a group of order \(2n\), so \(p\equiv-1\bmod16\). At such a prime \(J'\) has a rational half. For a primitive sixteenth root \(\zeta\) in \(\mathbb F_{p^2}\), put \(t=\zeta^2+\zeta^{-2}\), \(w=\zeta+\zeta^{-1}\). These are rational over \(\mathbb F_p\), with \(t^2=2\) and \(w^2=2+t\). The point \((4t,16w)\) on \(E'\) doubles to \(J'\). Meanwhile the non-\(J'\) point \([n/2]R\) is also divisible by two in \(E'(\mathbb F_p)\). Rational halves of two independent two-torsion points would give full rational four-torsion, hence \(\mu_4\subset\mathbb F_p\) by the Weil pairing. This contradicts \(p\equiv-1\bmod16\).

At a split prime of good reduction Frobenius is a complex multiplier \(\pi\) of norm \(p\). This follows because it commutes with the rationally defined CM action at this split place and the order \(\mathbb Z[\tau]\) is maximal. Since Frobenius fixes \(R\), the exact annihilator gives \(\pi\equiv1\bmod(n)\). Hence
\[
 p=(1+na)^2+2n^2b^2,\qquad a,b\in\mathbb Z,\quad b\ne0.
\]
In particular \(p\ge2n^2+1\). [CM action and denominators](abc_octic_mixed_cm_prime_mass.md)

## An upper sieve for the possible prime factors

Uniformly in dyadic \(n\), the number of distinct primes of this shape up to \(X\), with \(L=\sqrt X/n\ge2\), is \(O(L^2/\log(2L))\). Here is a derivation. All pairs are in a square box of side \(H\asymp L\). For odd \(\ell\), the norm form vanishes at \(\rho(\ell)=2\ell-1\) pairs modulo \(\ell\) if \(\ell\) splits, and at just one pair otherwise. This follows by the invertible coordinate change to \((1+na,nb)\). For odd squarefree \(d\), extend \(\rho\) multiplicatively. The count of pairs for which \(d\) divides the norm is
\[
 H^2 g(d)+O(2^{\omega(d)}(H+d)),\qquad g(d)=\rho(d)/d^2,
\]
by counting the individual residue classes inside the box.

For completeness use squarefree Selberg weights supported at odd \(d\le z=H^{1/8}\). Write \(h_1(\ell)=g(\ell)/(1-g(\ell))\) and extend multiplicatively. Put \(G(z)=\sum_{d\le z}h_1(d)\), over odd squarefree integers. The usual squared-weight upper bound gives a main term \(H^2/G(z)\) and error \(O((H+z^2)z^2\log^5(2z))\). Indeed, on writing \(y_r=\sum_{r\mid d}\lambda_d g(d)\), the main quadratic form is \(\sum_r y_r^2/h_1(r)\). Choose \(y_r=\mu(r)h_1(r)/G(z)\) and invert. This gives \(\lambda_1=1\) and \(|\lambda_d|\le1\), the latter by multiplying every index coprime to \(d\) up to \(z/d\) by all divisors of \(d\) in the sum for \(G(z)\). Each squarefree remainder of level \(q\le z^2\) occurs at most \(3^{\omega(q)}\) times. The error estimate follows from \(6^{\omega(q)}\le \tau_6(q)\), the six-fold divisor function.

It remains to bound \(G(z)\) below. For split primes \(\ell\le z^\theta\), the unrestricted product \(\prod(1+h_1(\ell))\asymp\log(z^\theta)\) follows from Mertens' theorem for the two reduced classes \(1,3\bmod8\). In this product the weighted mean of \(\log d\) is \(\sum g(\ell)\log\ell\ll\theta\log z\). Choosing a small fixed \(\theta>0\) leaves at least half the weight at \(d\le z\). Thus \(G(z)\gg\log(2z)\). Prime values up to \(z\) add at most \(z\) distinct primes. This proves the stated uniform bound.

For \(X\ge n^3\), the resulting total log weight of distinct eligible primes up to \(X\) is \(O(X/n^2)\), since \(\log X/\log(2L)\) is bounded. Along a sequence with \(P^+(w_n)=o(n^4)\), take \(X=\max(n^3,P^+(w_n))\). Then \(\log\operatorname{rad}(w_n)=o(n^2)\). In the whole-triple factorization \(w_n\mid s_{n-1}\mid T_{n-1}\) and \(T_{n-1}\ll c_{n-1}\). Hence
\[
 \log(c_{n-1}/\operatorname{rad}(a_{n-1}b_{n-1}c_{n-1}))
 \ge \log(w_n/\operatorname{rad}(w_n))-O(1)
 =\tfrac12h n^2-o(n^2).
\]
Finally \(\widehat h(A)=2h\) and \(\log c_{n-1}=4\widehat h(A)(n-1)^2+O(1)\). Dividing the two estimates gives the claimed quality limsup of at least \(16/15\).

## Finite checks

An exhaustive lattice calculation tested the cutoff \(n^4\) at \(n=8,16,\ldots,8192\). The single term \(w_8=2273\) is below its cutoff. Every tested \(n\ge16\) has a prime factor larger than \(n^4\). One can certify that assertion without factoring the large remaining cofactor: enumerate the possible norms up to \(n^4\), screen out values with a certified small divisor, and take the gcd of the product with \(w_n\). Remove every power of the primes occurring in that gcd. Any nontrivial remainder must then have a prime factor beyond the cutoff. At \(n=8192\), \(w_n\) has 3,498,318 decimal digits. Its only prime factor at or below \(8192^4\) is \(65229783041\), of exponent one. The rest of the integer is unfactored.
