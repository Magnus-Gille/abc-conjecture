# The only square denominator on the odd congruent-six orbit

Let \(G=(-3,9)\) on \(Y^2=X^3-36X\), and let \(B_n\) be the positive square root of the reduced abscissa denominator of \([n]G\). For odd \(n\),
\[
                          B_n\text{ is a square}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |n|=1.
\]
Equivalently, the primitive solutions with positive \(a,z\) to \(a^4+3b^2=4z^8\) consist of \((a,b,z)=(1,\pm1,1)\). The [elliptic parametrization](abc_full_quartic_signature.md) identifies \(w=B_n\), gives \(a,w,b\) odd and pairwise coprime with \(3\nmid aw\), and identifies the trivial solution with \(n=\pm1\). The proof below determines all rational points on a plane quartic by a rank-one elliptic computation over a quadratic field. Its finite calculations use [this standard-library verifier](abc_full_quartic_square_classification.py).

## A real quadratic descent

Take \(K=\mathbf Q(\sqrt3)\), \(\epsilon=2+\sqrt3\). The ring of integers \(\mathbf Z[\sqrt3]\) has class number one by the Minkowski bound \(\sqrt{12}/2<2\). Its units are \(\pm\epsilon^j\); the negative Pell equation is impossible modulo 3, and \(2+\sqrt3\) is the least real unit greater than 1. The conjugate factors in
\[
 (2z^4+b\sqrt3)(2z^4-b\sqrt3)=a^4
\]
are totally positive and have coprime ideals. A common prime must lie over a prime dividing \(a\), which excludes 2 and 3; then subtraction forces it to divide \(b\), contradicting coprimality. Consequently one factor is \(\epsilon^j(U+V\sqrt3)^4\) with integers \(U,V\). Their opposite parity follows from the odd norm. The rational coefficient of \((U+V\sqrt3)^4\) is odd, and its irrational coefficient is even. Comparing the rational coefficient \(2z^4\) shows that \(j\) is odd, as \(\epsilon\equiv\sqrt3\bmod2\) and \(\epsilon^2\equiv1\bmod2\). Conjugating if needed and absorbing fourth powers of \(\epsilon\) permits \(j=1\). Taking the rational coefficient and the norm gives
\[
 C:\quad Z^4=U^4+6U^3V+18U^2V^2+18UV^3+9V^4,\qquad a=|U^2-3V^2|,
\]
with \(Z=z\). We prove \(C(\mathbf Q)=\{(1:0:1),(1:0:-1)\}\).

## A rank-one elliptic curve over \(K\)

Put \(X=(U+\sqrt3 V)/Z\), \(Y=(U-\sqrt3 V)/Z\). Then \(\epsilon X^4+\epsilon^{-1}Y^4=4\); every real point has \(Z\ne0\) by positivity of the left side before division. The map
\[
       Q=(-Y^2,\epsilon X^2Y)\in J(K),\qquad
       J:\quad y^2=x^3+A x,\quad A=-4\epsilon,
\]
takes the two known points to \(\pm P\), where \(P=(-1,\epsilon)\). Indeed \((\epsilon X^2)^2=4\epsilon-Y^4\).

At the two inert primes 5 and 7 the good reduced groups have orders 32 and 36. Hence \(J(K)_{\rm tors}\) is a 2-group of order at most four, by prime-to-residue-characteristic injectivity. There is exactly one nonzero rational two-torsion point \(T=(0,0)\). In fact \(\epsilon\) is not a square in \(K\), since \(\epsilon=(1+\sqrt3)^2/2\) and \(\sqrt2\notin K\). No rational point doubles to \(T\), since the duplication numerator would require \(x^2=A<0\) at a real place. Thus the torsion subgroup is exactly \(\{O,T\}\).

For the ordinary two-isogeny descent use the abscissa squareclass map on \(J\) and its dual \(J': y^2=x^3+16\epsilon x\). Only the prime generated by \(\delta=1+\sqrt3\), of norm \(-2\), can have odd valuation in either descent image. This follows directly by comparing the valuations of \(y^2=x(x^2+A)\) at a prime where \(A\) is a unit, and likewise on the dual. Since the class number is one and \(\delta^2=2\epsilon\), both images lie in \(\langle-1,\epsilon,\delta\rangle\) modulo squares. On \(J'\) the abscissa is positive at both real places unless it is zero; the torsion value is the totally positive class \(\epsilon\). The distinct sign patterns of \(-1,\delta\) force the image to be exactly \(\{1,\epsilon\}\).

On \(J\), the points \(T,P\) provide the classes \(-\epsilon,-1\). For a representative \(e\) with \(v_\delta(e)=1\), the descent equation \(s^2=e u^4+(A/e)v^4\) at a primitive local pair has right-hand valuation 1 if \(u\) is a unit and 3 otherwise, since \(v_\delta(A)=4\). Hence the image is exactly \(\langle-1,\epsilon\rangle\) of order four. The two-isogeny rank formula \(2^{\mathrm{rank}}=|\alpha(J)||\alpha(J')|/4\) gives \(\mathrm{rank}\,J(K)=1\). Moreover \(P\) has odd index modulo torsion in the free part: neither \(-1\) nor its product with the torsion class \(-\epsilon\) is trivial, so \(P\notin2J(K)+J(K)_{\rm tors}\).

## Reduction at 7 and local uniqueness

Write \(k=\mathbf F_7[\sqrt3]\). Direct enumeration gives that all 36 points of \(J(k)\) are killed by 6, that \(P\) reduces to order 6, and that \(3\bar P=\bar T\). In particular the index of \(P\) modulo torsion is prime to 3: if divisible by 3, its reduction would have order dividing 2. Since this index is also odd, the reduction of \(J(K)\) is exactly \(\langle\bar P\rangle\). The eight points of \(C(\mathbf F_7)\) are
\[
  (0:1:\pm2),\quad(4:1:\pm1),\quad(6:1:\pm3),\quad(1:0:\pm1).
\]
The verifier evaluates \(Q\) at all eight; only \((1:0:\pm1)\) map into \(\langle\bar P\rangle\). All rational points therefore lie in the two residue disks of the displayed rational points.

Work in \(K_7=\mathbf Q_7(\sqrt3)\), with ring of integers \(\mathcal O_7\), and write \(\log_J\) for the formal logarithm normalized by \(dx/(2y)\). Its restriction to the formal group is injective. On the disk of \((1:0:Z_0)\), \(Z_0=\pm1\), set \(U=1\), \(t=V\in7\mathbf Z_7\), and choose the analytic branch \(Z(t)\) with \(Z(0)=Z_0\). Its power series is integral at 7 since \(4 Z_0^3\) is a unit. At \(t=0\), \(Z'/Z=3/2\) and \(x(Q(t))'=3+2\sqrt3\). The tangent logarithm of the image is therefore
\[
            \frac{x'(0)}{2y(0)}=\pm\frac{3+2\sqrt3}{2\epsilon}
                                =\pm\frac{\sqrt3}{2}.
\]
The pullback of the differential has integral power series on the disk. On integrating, every term of degree \(j\ge2\) has valuation at least \(jv_7(t)-v_7(j)>v_7(t)\) for \(t\ne0\). Thus \(\log_J(Q(t)-Q(0))/t\) reduces to the nonzero purely irrational residue \(\pm\sqrt3/2\) in \(k\).

The rank-one global logarithm line has a different reduction. Addition modulo 49 gives
\[
                  x(3P)=0\bmod49,\qquad y(3P)=35\sqrt3\bmod49.
\]
Near \(T\) the coordinate \(y\) is a parameter and the invariant differential is \(dy/(3x^2+A)\). Its integral from \(T\) to \(3P\) is \(y(3P)/A\bmod49\). Since \(2T=O\),
\[
        \log_J(6P)\equiv2 y(3P)/A\equiv28+14\sqrt3\pmod{49},
        \qquad \log_J(6P)/7\equiv4+2\sqrt3\pmod7.
\]
For any rational point in the disk, \(Q(t)-Q(0)\) is a global point of \(J(K)\) in the formal group. As \(J(K)\) has rank one, its logarithm lies in the \(\mathbf Q_7\)-line generated by \(\log_J(6P)\). At \(t\ne0\) the normalized tangent has a unit residue, so equality of these lines would make \(\pm\sqrt3/2\) and \(4+2\sqrt3\) proportional over \(\mathbf F_7\). They are independent since the latter has constant coefficient 4. Therefore \(t=0\) in either disk. This determines \(C(\mathbf Q)\). Now \(V=0\) gives \(z=|U|\) and \(a=U^2\); coprimality forces \(z=a=1\), and the original equation gives \(b=\pm1\).
