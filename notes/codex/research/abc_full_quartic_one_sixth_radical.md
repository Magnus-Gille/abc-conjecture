# An effective one-sixth exponent at every index

For every positive odd index on $a_n^4+3(c_nd_n)^2=C_n=4w_n^4$, put $R_n=\operatorname{rad}(6a_nc_nd_nw_n)$. Then
$$
 \operatorname{rad}(a_nw_n)\gg \frac{n^6}{(\log(2n))^6},\qquad
 \log C_n\ll R_n^{1/6}(\log(2R_n))^{5/2}.
$$
The constants are effectively computable. The four coordinates are positive, odd and pairwise coprime. At odd indices $w_n,a_n$ are the denominator roots of $[n]G,[n]S$ respectively, on $E:Y^2=X^3-36X$, where $G=(-3,9)$ and $S=G+(0,0)=(12,36)$. Both points have canonical height $H>0$. At every sufficiently large odd $n$, $\log a_n\gg n^2$ effectively by complex elliptic logarithms, and $\log w_n=Hn^2+O(1)$. All their primes exceed 3. A prime of rational rank $r$ in either arm exceeds $2r$ for odd $r>1$ by full rational two-torsion and Hasse. Every prime of $w_n$ is $1\bmod 12$. ## Gaussian divisors

The curve has complex multiplication by $\mathbf Z[i]$ over $K=\mathbf Q(i)$. At every sufficiently large prime $q=\lambda\bar\lambda\equiv1\bmod4$, for each of $Z=G,S$ there is a good place where the Gaussian annihilator of $Z$ is $(\lambda)$. This is the bounded-content Gaussian denominator estimate, specialized to the primitive multiplier $\lambda=u+iv$ of norm $q$. For clarity, the complex torus distances of $[\lambda]Z$ and $[\bar\lambda]Z$ to the origin are equal. Their sum and difference imply that $[2u]Z,[2v]Z$ are both within twice this distance of the origin, using the isometry $[i]$. Since $\gcd(u,v)=1$, Bézout coefficients of size $O(\sqrt q)$ express the fixed nonzero point $[2]Z$ as a combination. Thus $\log^+|X([\lambda]Z)|=O(\log q)$. The canonical height is $qH$, hence the log norm of the denominator ideal is $\gg q$. The places above 2 and 3 contribute only $O(1)$: after multiplication by a fixed scalar taking $Z$ deep into the formal group, the formal logarithm commutes with $[\lambda]$ and $\lambda$ is a unit there. At positive denominator depth a multiplication by an integer can only increase the depth. A good place therefore divides the denominator. Its annihilator contains the prime ideal $(\lambda)$, and cannot be the whole ring since $Z$ is integral. All bounds here are effective. The underlying rational prime $p$ divides the corresponding rational denominator at index $q$, has order $q$, and exceeds $2q$. The differential of $[\bar\lambda]$ on the local formal group is a unit at this place, so its depth at $[q]Z$ equals that at $[\lambda]Z$. In particular $v_p(B([q]Z))\log p\ll q$, by the global height bound on $[\lambda]Z$. Here $B$ denotes the root of the rational abscissa denominator. For $Z=S$, this prime cannot account for all of $a_q$, since $\log a_q\gg q^2$. Thus at every sufficiently large split prime index, $a_q$ has two or more distinct primes.

For any prime $q$ and fixed $1\le j\le4$, write $m=q^j$. For all sufficiently large $q$, there is a place of exact Gaussian annihilator $(m)$ for $G$. Indeed every prime of $w_m$ has rational rank a positive power of $q$, hence exceeds $2q$. If a place above such a prime has proper early annihilator $(\beta)$ dividing $(m)$, its denominator depth at $[m]G$ equals its depth at $[\beta]G$, since the multiplier $m/\beta$ is a local unit. There are at most $(j+1)^2$ Gaussian ideal divisors of $(m)$, and every proper one has norm at most $m^2/q$. Each of their denominator ideals has log norm $O(N\beta)$ by the canonical height. They cannot account for $\log w_m\asymp m^2$. This proves existence effectively. The resulting rational prime $h$ is $1\bmod 12$ and its ordinary Frobenius is a Gaussian integer $\pi$ of norm $h$. The equality $[\pi]G=G$ on reduction implies $\pi-1\in(m)$; the nonzero imaginary part of $\pi$ is therefore divisible by $m$. Hence $h\ge m^2$. Its rational rank is exactly $m$. For $m=q$ split, this rational prime differs from the prime at annihilator $(\lambda)$, because conjugate places above a rational prime have conjugate annihilators and $(q)$ is fixed by conjugation.

## Prime indices

If $q\equiv1\bmod4$, the two distinct Gaussian primes just exhibited for the $w$ coordinate show $\operatorname{rad}(w_q)\gg q^3$. For $a_q$ there are at least two primes. Each exceeds $2q$, and in the case of exactly two the [quartic unit-equation estimate](abc_full_quartic_composite_radical.md) gives one $\gg q^2/(\log(2q))^6$. With three primes their product already exceeds $q^3$. This proves the desired product estimate at split primes.

If $q\equiv3\bmod4$, every prime $p\mid w_q$ exceeds or equals $q^2$. Indeed $q$ is inert in the Gaussian integers, so rational order $q$ forces Gaussian annihilator $(q)$, and again $\pi\equiv1\bmod q$. If at least two primes divide $w_q$, use $\operatorname{rad}(w_q)\ge q^4$ and $\operatorname{rad}(a_q)\gg q^2/(\log(2q))^3$. If $w_q$ is prime, its exponential size suffices. [Uniform bound on the $a$-coordinate](abc_full_quartic_one_fifth_radical.md)

It remains to treat $w_q=p^f$ with $f>1$. The square and cube classifications and the [Eisenstein exponent descent](abc_full_quartic_prime_exponents.md) imply that each prime $\ell\mid f$ is at least 47 and divides $a_q$, so $\ell>2q$. Since $f\log p=Hq^2+O(1)$, this makes $f$ itself prime for all sufficiently large $q$. The descent yields coprime conjugate algebraic integers $\gamma,\bar\gamma$ of norm $p$ in $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-3})$, and
$$
                   U^f+V^f=-a_q^2,\qquad U=\gamma^4,\quad V=\bar\gamma^4.
$$
The Lucas pair $U,V$ has nonzero coprime rational integers $U+V,UV$, and $U/V$ cannot be a root of unity because its numerator and denominator generate distinct coprime nonunit ideals. By Theorem 1.4 of Bilu, Hanrot and Voutier, the Lucas number $L_{2f}=(U^{2f}-V^{2f})/(U-V)$ has a primitive rational prime divisor $h$, as $2f>30$. Such a prime divides neither $(U-V)^2$ nor any earlier Lucas number. From $L_{2f}=L_f(U^f+V^f)$ it follows that $h\mid a_q$. At a place over $h$, the ratio $U/V$ has exact multiplicative order $2f$, since $U,V,U-V$ are units there. Frobenius either fixes the ratio or inverts it, giving $2f\mid h-1$ or $2f\mid h+1$. In particular $h\ge2f-1>f$. Since also $f\mid a_q$, one obtains $\operatorname{rad}(a_qw_q)\ge pf(2f-1)$. If $p\ge q^6$ this suffices. Otherwise $f\gg q^2/\log(2q)$, and $p\ge q^2$, proving the bound even with a logarithmic square. [Bilu, Hanrot and Voutier, *Existence of primitive divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers*, J. Reine Angew. Math. 539 (2001), 75–122](https://doi.org/10.1515/crll.2001.080)

## Composite indices and the full radical

If $\tau(n)\ge6$, the primitive-divisor product on each of the two arms gives $\operatorname{rad}(a_nw_n)\gg n^{\tau(n)}\ge n^6$. At $n=qr$ for distinct primes, all primes at ranks $q,r,n$ are distinct on either arm, and the two arms are coprime. The prime-index bound at $q,r$ gives $\gg n^6/(\log(2n))^{12}$. Two additional primes of exact rank $n$, one on each arm, each exceed $n$, absorbing the logarithmic factor. [Primitive-divisor product](abc_full_quartic_divisor_sensitive_radical.md)

Every remaining composite index is $n=q^b$, $2\le b\le4$. Assume $q$ above the fixed effective cutoff, the other cases having bounded $n$. Each rank $q^j$ gives a primitive prime of $a$ exceeding $q^j$, and a primitive prime of $w$ at least $q^{2j}$ by the full Gaussian annihilator. For $b=3,4$ their product over $1\le j\le b$ is at least $n^{3(b+1)/2}\ge n^6$. For $b=2$ use the established prime-index bound $\gg q^6/(\log(2q))^6$ at level one and the new primes of product at least $n^3$ at level two. This finishes the first displayed inequality.

Finally set $U_0=2w_n-c_n$ and $V_0=2w_n+c_n$. The primitive triple $U_0+2c_n=V_0$ has $U_0 V_0=3d_n^2$, so its radical is at most $6\operatorname{rad}(c_nd_n)$ and $V_0>2w_n$. Stewart–Yu implies $\operatorname{rad}(c_nd_n)\gg n^6/(\log(2R_n))^9$. It also gives $\log(2n)\ll\log(2R_n)$. Multiplying the coprime products and using $\log C_n\asymp n^2$ proves the assertion.
